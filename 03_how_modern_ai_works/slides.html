In plain words

Computers follow rules. The simplest kind of rule is an if/then statement: if something is true, do this; otherwise, do that. You used them in Python in Workshop 1 (for example, marking a trial correct or wrong). Every program is built from lots of these.

Try the animals above. The rules work for the cases the programmer thought of and fail on the rest: a cat and a robot dog both get “not sure”, and a bat gets called a bird. 1936: the Turing machine

1 · The tape

A long strip of cells, each holding one symbol. It is the machine’s memory.

2 · The head

Reads the symbol in one cell, writes a new one, then moves one cell left or right.

3 · The rules

A table of if/then lines: if I’m in this state and read this symbol, then write, move, and switch state.

In plain words

In 1936, the mathematician Alan Turing asked: what does it mean to “compute” something? His answer was an imaginary machine so simple you could build it from paper and a pencil: a tape (memory), a head that reads and writes one cell at a time, and a table of rules. Each rule is an if/then: if I’m in state A and I read a 1, then write a 0, move right and switch to state B.

The big discovery: one machine, given the right rules, can imitate any other machine. That is the idea of software: the same hardware can run many different programs. Everything a laptop or phone does, from a Python script to a chatbot, rests on this idea.

Check yourself: where have you already seen “if this, then that” in Workshops 1 and 2?

Next · 1943

What if we copied the brain instead?

In plain words

This machine adds 1 to a number written in binary (the 0s and 1s computers use). It first walks right until it reaches the end of the number. Then it walks back left, turning every 1 into a 0 (that is the “carry”) until it finds a 0 or a blank, and turns that into a 1. The highlighted row in the table is the rule that will fire on the next step.

Notice what is not there: no understanding of numbers, only a table of if/then rules. Yet this tiny machine really does arithmetic. The limit is that a person had to write every rule in advance.

In plain words

In 1943, the neuroscientist Warren McCulloch and the logician Walter Pitts proposed that a brain cell could be modelled as a tiny calculator. It receives several inputs, each counts more or less (its weight), it adds them up, and it “fires” if the total is high enough. Their version was strictly on or off; modern neurons give a smooth score between 0 and 1 (the sigmoid curve).

It is a very simple cartoon of a real neuron, which is far more complicated. But it turned out to be a useful building block: connect many of them together and you get a neural network.

Next · 1958

A neuron that chooses its own weights.

In plain words

In 1950 Turing asked whether a machine could ever think. Rather than argue about the word “think”, he proposed a practical test: a judge chats by text with a hidden person and a hidden machine; if the judge can’t tell them apart, the machine passes. We now call this the Turing test.

In 1956 a group of researchers held a summer workshop at Dartmouth College and gave the field a name: artificial intelligence. Now there is a goal (conversation, reasoning, learning) and a promising building block (the neuron). What is missing is a way for the machine to find its own weights.

Chapter 4 · 1958

1958: the perceptron learns from examples

Traditional programming

Rules + data ▶ answers

A person writes the rules: “If the email says free money, mark it as spam.”

Machine learning

Data + answers ▶ rules

Show it thousands of emails already labeled spam or not, and let it work out the patterns itself.

Frank Rosenblatt’s perceptron: a neuron that adjusts its own weights.

In plain words

This is the most important idea in the guide. Remember the if/then animal checker: a person had to write every rule, and it broke on cats and robot dogs. In 1958 Frank Rosenblatt built the perceptron, a neuron that sets its own weights. Instead of writing rules, you show it data and the right answers and it works out the rules itself. This is called machine learning.

The catch: the “rules” it ends up with are lists of numbers (weights), not sentences a person can read.

Chapter 4 · 1958

Learning means nudging weights downhill

Height = how wrong the model is. Each dot is one training step.

1

Guess. Run an example through the neuron.

2

Measure the error. Compare the guess with the right answer.

3

Repeat for every example, many times.

In plain words

Training means finding good weights. The neuron makes a guess, we measure how wrong it was (the error), and every weight is nudged a tiny bit in the direction that makes the error smaller. Then we repeat with the next example.

Picture a foggy hillside. You can’t see the valley floor, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a small step downhill, and repeat. This is called gradient descent. The next slide lets you watch it happen.

Next · 1966

ELIZA, a conversation made of if/thens.

In plain words

Here a very small neuron learns “is it a dog?” from five examples. It starts with every weight at zero, so it knows nothing and says 50% for everything. In each round it sees all five examples; for each one it compares its guess with the right answer and nudges the weights a little.

Nobody tells it that barking means dog. It discovers that from the examples, and you can watch the Barks weight grow while the Wings weight turns negative.

Chapter 6 · 1969

1969: one neuron can’t do everything

■ answer 1   □ answer 0. No single straight line separates them.

The puzzle: XOR

The answer is 1 when exactly one input is on, and 0 when both or neither are.

Why one neuron fails

A single neuron can only draw one straight line between “yes” and “no”.

What happened next

Minsky and Papert’s book Perceptrons (1969) pointed this out. Interest and funding faded: the “AI winters”.

In plain words

In 1969 Marvin Minsky and Seymour Papert published a book analysing what perceptrons can and cannot do. One thing they cannot do is XOR (“exclusive or”): output 1 when exactly one of two inputs is on. Plot the four cases and you can see why: a single neuron separates the world with one straight line, and no straight line splits the filled corners from the hollow ones.

The book made many people lose faith in neural networks, and funding and interest faded for years. These quiet periods are known as the AI winters. The fix was already hinted at: use more than one layer of neurons. The next slide lets you try it.

Next · 1986

Backpropagation.

In plain words

With one neuron, you have three dials (two weights and a bias). Drag them around: you will never get all four cases right, because a single neuron can only draw one straight line. The best you can do is three out of four.

Now switch on Add a hidden layer. Here the weights are already set for you: one hidden neuron spots “at least one input is on” (OR), another spots “both inputs are on” (AND), and the output says “OR, but not AND”. Two layers solve XOR. That is why networks have layers.

Check yourself: can you get four out of four with one neuron? Why not?

Chapter 7 · 1986

1986: backpropagation trains many layers

Forward

The input flows through the layers and the network makes a guess.

Backward

The error flows back through the layers. Each weight learns how much it contributed, and is nudged.

Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams (1986): the same downhill nudge as 1958, through every layer.

In plain words

In 1958 a neuron could learn its weights. But with two layers, a hidden neuron never sees the right answer directly: how should its weights change? Backpropagation answers that. After the network makes a guess, the error is passed backwards through the layers, and each weight is told how much it contributed to the mistake, so each one can be nudged downhill just like before.

The method existed in earlier forms, but a 1986 paper by David Rumelhart, Geoffrey Hinton and Ronald Williams made it widely known. With it, multi-layer networks could finally be trained.

Next · 2012

They arrive together.

In plain words

In 1997 IBM’s Deep Blue beat the world chess champion Garry Kasparov. It was a triumph of rules (written by experts) and sheer computing speed, not of learning. Rules were at their peak.

Neural networks, meanwhile, were waiting for three things. They needed methods (backpropagation, already there), a lot of data (the internet and big labelled datasets such as ImageNet, 2009), and much faster chips (graphics processors, or GPUs, built for video games but ideal for the maths of networks).

In plain words

In 2012 a deep neural network called AlexNet won an image-recognition contest by a wide margin. It had 8 learned layers and about 60 million weights, and was trained on GPUs using millions of labelled photos. The same ingredients that had been waiting (methods, data, chips) finally came together, and the field changed direction.

“Deep” just means many layers. Roughly speaking, early layers pick up simple things like edges, later ones combine them into shapes, and the last ones recognise objects. Nobody programmed those stages; the network found them while training. This picture is a simplification.

Four steps, in the order the data flows. Each one has a slide you can play with.

In plain words

A network works on numbers, so to use one on language you have to turn text into numbers first, and turn numbers back into text at the end. Between 2013 and 2017 researchers worked out a recipe in four steps, and this is still the core of every chatbot: tokens (cut up the text), embeddings (give each piece coordinates), attention (let pieces look at each other) and prediction (guess what comes next).

The next four slides take these in order. Click a card to jump to it.

Try it · Step 1
1 Tokens▶2 Embeddings▶3 Attention▶4 Prediction

Step 1: text becomes tokens

In plain words

Computers only work with numbers, so text has to be converted first. It is chopped into pieces called tokens (whole words, parts of words, or punctuation), and each piece is replaced by an ID number from a fixed list. Common words get a token of their own; rare words are split into reusable pieces. A token is roughly three-quarters of an English word.

This demo uses a tiny made-up vocabulary, so the splits and ID numbers are only illustrative. Real models have vocabularies of tens or hundreds of thousands of pieces. One consequence: because the model sees tokens and not letters, it can be surprisingly bad at tasks like counting the letters in a word.

Check yourself: type a made-up word. Which pieces does it get split into, and why?

In plain words

A token ID on its own is just a label. So each token is next turned into a long list of numbers, called an embedding, which you can think of as coordinates on a map of meaning. During training, words that are used in similar ways get pushed to similar places on the map.

That makes meaning behave like geometry: “cat” sits near “dog”, and the step from “king” to “queen” points the same way as the step from “man” to “woman” (a famous 2013 result, from a method called word2vec). Real maps have thousands of dimensions; this two-dimensional picture is only a drawing of the idea.

Check yourself: click “king”. Which words are its nearest neighbours, and does that make sense?

Step 3: attention reads the context

Change one word and the meaning of “it” shifts. Attention is the heart of the transformer (2017), the design behind today’s chatbots. (Hand-made illustration, not real model values.)

In plain words

Words get their meaning from their context. In “it was too tired”, “it” means the animal; in “it was too wide”, “it” means the street. Attention is the mechanism that lets every word look at every other word and decide which ones matter for understanding it.

Before 2017, language models read one word at a time and struggled to connect words far apart. In 2017 Google researchers published “Attention Is All You Need”, introducing the transformer: many layers of attention combined with the neurons you already know. Real models run many attention “heads” at once, so they track grammar, references and topic together.

Next · 2020

The same recipe, scaled up.

In plain words

This is the core loop of every chatbot. Given all the tokens so far, the network outputs a probability for every possible next token. One is picked, added to the text, and the whole process runs again. A reply is simply this loop repeated, one token at a time.

The picking is a little random. The randomness slider (technically called temperature) controls how adventurous it is: at the low end it nearly always takes the most likely word, which gets repetitive; at the high end it takes more surprising words. The model here is a tiny hand-made one with made-up probabilities that only looks at the last word; real models look at the whole text through attention.

Check yourself: turn the randomness to the minimum and press Pick for me several times. What happens? Now try the maximum. Notice that the sentences are fluent but nothing checks that they are true.

A pretrained model only continues text. Ask it “What is the capital of France?” and it may reply with more quiz questions, because lists of quiz questions are common online. Click through the three tabs to see how each stage changes the reply.

Next · today

Where it goes wrong.

In plain words

The same recipe works for other kinds of data: images are cut into patches and sound into short slices, and each becomes tokens. Models can also be given tools: the model writes a request such as “run this Python code”, software outside the model carries it out, and the result is pasted back in as more tokens.

That is how a chatbot can analyse a spreadsheet like the one from Workshop 1: it writes and runs code, rather than doing the arithmetic in its head.

Chapter 13 · Today

Four ways it goes wrong

Hallucination

Confident, fluent and false, like a citation to a paper that doesn’t exist.

Bias

It learns the patterns in its data, including the unfair ones.

Knowledge cutoff

It knows nothing after its training data ends, unless a tool looks it up.

No built-in fact check

Training rewards plausible text. Plausible and true usually overlap, but not always.

In plain words

All four problems follow from how the model works, which you have now seen. It always produces the most plausible continuation, so a made-up citation can look as convincing as a real one (hallucination). It copies the patterns in its data, including unfair ones (bias). Its weights freeze when training ends (cutoff). And nothing in training rewards “true”, only “plausible”. Remember the toy generator two chapters ago: its sentences were fluent whether or not they made sense.

Ground rules for the lab: check citations, statistics and methods details against the original source; test any AI-written code on made-up data and check the output by hand; and never paste participant data into a public chatbot.

The one thing to remember

Fluent isn’t the same as true.

Treat an AI answer as a draft to check, not a source to cite.

In plain words

If you remember only one thing from this guide, make it this. These systems are extraordinarily good at producing text that sounds right, because that is exactly what they were trained to do. Sounding right and being right usually go together, which is why they are so useful, but the model has no separate step that checks the truth.

Checking is your job.

Recap

The whole story in one slide

Today: fluent isn’t the same as true.

In plain words

Here is the whole chain. People first built machines from rules (1936), copied the neuron (1943), set the goal (1950), let the neuron learn (1958), found the limit of one neuron (1969), and fixed it by training many layers (1986). Data and chips arrived (1997–2012) and deep learning took off. A recipe for language (2013–17), enormous scale (2020) and extra training (2022) produced the chatbots of today, with their one big weakness. Click any item to revisit that chapter.
