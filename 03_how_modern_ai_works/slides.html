Pick a preset above to see whether the rules get it right.

In plain words

An if/then statement is the simplest kind of decision a computer makes: if something is true, do this; otherwise, do that. You used them in Python in Workshop 1 (for example, marking a trial correct or wrong). Every program is built from lots of these.

Try the animals above. The rules work for the cases the programmer thought of, and fail on the rest: a cat and a robot dog both get “not sure”, and a bat gets called a bird. To fix that, a person has to write more rules, and the world never runs out of exceptions.

Check yourself: what rule would you add so cats work? What new problem might that rule create?

Talk to it
In plain words

ELIZA (1966) played a therapist using nothing but if/then rules: look for a keyword, then fill in a template. People were startled by how real it felt, even though it understood nothing at all. The mini version above works the same way.

Now break it: ask it a factual question, or say something that matches no rule. It deflects with “Please, go on.” Hand-written rules cannot cover everything people might say, and modern chatbots do not work like this. The rest of the guide explains how they do work.

Check yourself: how many rules would you need to answer any question in English?

A Turing machine: a tape, a head, and rules

1 · The tape

A long strip of cells, each holding one symbol. It is the machine’s memory.

2 · The head

Reads the symbol in one cell, writes a new one, then moves one cell left or right.

3 · The rules

A table of if/then lines: if I’m in this state and read this symbol, then write, move, and switch state.

In plain words

In 1936, the mathematician Alan Turing asked: what does it mean to “compute” something? His answer was an imaginary machine so simple you could build it from paper and a pencil: a tape (memory), a head that reads and writes one cell at a time, and a table of rules. Each rule is an if/then: if I’m in state A and I read a 1, then write a 0, move right and switch to state B.

The big discovery: one machine, given the right rules, can imitate any other machine. That is the idea of software: the same hardware can run many different programs. Everything a laptop or phone does, from a Python script to a chatbot, rests on this idea.

Check yourself: where have you already seen “if this, then that” in Workshops 1 and 2?

Run a Turing machine yourself

Its rules: this machine adds 1 to a binary number
In stateReadsWritesMovesNew state
In plain words

This machine adds 1 to a number written in binary (the 0s and 1s computers use). It first walks right until it reaches the end of the number. Then it walks back left, turning every 1 into a 0 (that is the “carry”) until it finds a 0 or a blank, and turns that into a 1. The highlighted row in the table is the rule that will fire on the next step.

Notice what is not there: no understanding of numbers, only a table of if/then rules. Yet this tiny machine really does arithmetic.

Check yourself: before pressing Run, predict the final tape for 111. (Answer: 1000.) Then try 1111 and 0.

Short history · Part 1

A short history, part 1: the ideas (1936–1966)

RULES = people write the instructions  ·  BRAIN-INSPIRED = machines modelled on neurons  ·  IDEA = concepts and goals

Rules

The Turing machine

Alan Turing describes a machine with a tape and a table of rules: the blueprint for every computer.

Brain-inspired

Idea

Idea
1956

“Artificial intelligence”

A summer workshop at Dartmouth College gives the field its name.

Brain-inspired
1958

The perceptron

Frank Rosenblatt builds a neuron that learns its own weights from examples.

Rules
1966

ELIZA

Joseph Weizenbaum’s chatbot runs on if/then rules. You just talked to a mini version.

In plain words

Notice two threads. One is people building machines that follow rules they were given (Turing, ELIZA). The other is people building machines modelled on the brain that learn from examples (McCulloch and Pitts, the perceptron). For decades the two threads competed.

Also notice how young the field is: the name “artificial intelligence” is only about 70 years old.

A short history, part 2: scale (1969–today)

RULES = people write the instructions  ·  BRAIN-INSPIRED = machines modelled on neurons  ·  IDEA = concepts and goals

Brain-inspired

Rules

A chess computer wins using hand-written rules and brute-force search, not learning.

Brain-inspired
2012

Deep learning takes off

AlexNet, a deep network trained on graphics chips, wins an image-recognition contest by a wide margin.

Brain-inspired
2017

The transformer

Brain-inspired

In plain words

Neural networks fell out of favour after 1969 because single neurons have limits and computers were too slow. Three things changed: a practical training method (backpropagation, 1986), far more data, and far faster chips (GPUs). In 2012 a deep neural network won an image-recognition contest by a wide margin and the field changed direction. In 2017 the transformer made it practical to learn from text at huge scale, and in 2022 ChatGPT put the result in everyone’s hands.

Other milestones you may have heard of: in 2016, AlphaGo beat a top human player at Go, having learned from human games and from playing against itself.

The big picture · Part 2

AI is a family of nested ideas

Artificial intelligence
Machine learning
Deep learning
LLMs

Artificial intelligence

Machines doing tasks we’d say need intelligence.

Machine learning

Learning patterns from examples, not hand-written rules.

Deep learning

Machine learning with large, many-layered neural networks.

Large language models (LLMs)

Deep networks trained on vast amounts of text.

In plain words

These words are often used as if they meant the same thing. They don’t. Each one is a smaller box inside the previous one. “AI” is the broad goal, and includes old rule-based systems like ELIZA and Deep Blue. “Machine learning” means the system learns its behaviour from examples. “Deep learning” means it does so with many-layered neural networks. “LLMs” are deep networks trained on text, such as ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini.

The key shift · Part 2

Machine learning flips programming around

Traditional programming

Rules + data ▶ answers

A person writes the rules: “If the email says free money, mark it as spam.”

Machine learning

Data + answers ▶ rules

Show it thousands of emails already labeled spam or not, and let it work out the patterns itself.

In plain words

This is the single most important idea in the guide. Remember the if/then dog detector from slide 4: a person had to write every rule, and it broke on cats and robot dogs. Machine learning flips the process. Instead of writing the rules, you give the computer data and the right answers, and it works out the rules itself.

The catch: the “rules” it ends up with are long lists of numbers (called weights), not sentences a person can read.

In plain words

A neuron is a tiny calculator with four steps. 1) Inputs: numbers describing something (is it furry? does it bark? does it have wings?). 2) Weights: each input is multiplied by a weight saying how much it matters; a positive weight is evidence for, a negative one is evidence against. 3) Add up the results. 4) Squash: turn the total into a score between 0 and 1 using a smooth curve called the sigmoid.

The idea was inspired by real brain cells, which also add up many inputs and “fire” when the total is high enough. But the artificial version is a very simple cartoon of the real thing.

Networks · Part 2

From one neuron to a network

Every square is a neuron doing the same add-up-and-squash.

Each line is a weight. This tiny network has 20 of them.

Early layers spot simple patterns; later layers combine them. Deep means many layers.

In plain words

One neuron can only combine its inputs in one simple way. Wiring many neurons into layers, where each layer feeds the next, lets a network build complicated ideas out of simple ones. In an image network, early layers pick up edges, later ones combine them into shapes, and later still into faces or dogs.

Every line in the diagram is a weight, so a network is simply a huge number of dials. This little one has 20. Large language models have billions.

Training · Part 2

Learning means nudging the weights downhill

Height = how wrong the model is. Each dot is one training step.

1

Guess. Run an example through the network.

2

Measure the error. Compare the guess with the right answer.

3

Nudge every weight a little in the direction that shrinks the error.

Repeat, billions of times.

In plain words

Training means finding good weights. The network makes a guess, we measure how wrong it was (the error), and every weight is nudged a tiny bit in the direction that makes the error smaller. Then we repeat with the next example.

Picture a foggy hillside. You can’t see the valley floor, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a small step downhill, and repeat. The “slope” is worked out by an algorithm called backpropagation. Real networks do this billions of times, with billions of weights.

In plain words

Here a very small neuron learns “is it a dog?” from five examples. It starts with every weight at zero, so it knows nothing and says 50% for everything. In each round it sees all five examples; for each one it compares its guess with the right answer and nudges the weights a little (up for inputs that should have pushed toward the right answer, down for the others).

Nobody tells it that barking means dog. It discovers that from the examples, and you can watch the Barks weight grow while the Wings weight turns negative.

Step 1 · Tokens · Part 3

First, text is chopped into tokens

“Students love unbelievably long lectures.”

Studentsloveunbelievablylonglectures.
▼
[ 9218, 812, 3021, 403, 7745, 1102, 1588, 6431, 984, 13 ]

The model never sees letters, only a list of numbers. One token is roughly ¾ of an English word. (Split and IDs are illustrative.)

In plain words

Computers only work with numbers, so text has to be converted first. It is chopped into pieces called tokens (whole words, parts of words, or punctuation), and each piece is replaced by an ID number from a fixed list. A token is roughly three-quarters of an English word.

The split and the numbers on this slide are made up to show the idea; every model has its own. One consequence: because the model sees tokens and not letters, it can be surprisingly bad at tasks like counting the letters in a word.

Step 2 · Embeddings · Part 3

Each token becomes a point in “meaning space”

animalsfoodpeople

Words used in similar ways land close together.

Directions carry meaning: king → queen is the same step as man → woman.

Real models use thousands of dimensions. We can only draw two.

In plain words

A token ID on its own is just a label. So each token is next turned into a long list of numbers, called an embedding, which you can think of as coordinates on a map of meaning. During training, words that are used in similar ways get pushed to similar places on the map.

That makes meaning behave like geometry: the step from “king” to “queen” points the same way as the step from “man” to “woman”. Real maps have thousands of dimensions; this two-dimensional picture is only a drawing of the idea.

In plain words

This is the core loop of every chatbot. Given all the tokens so far, the network outputs a probability for every possible next token. One is picked, added to the text, and the whole process runs again. A reply is simply this loop repeated, one token at a time.

A little randomness (called temperature) keeps the answers from being identical every time. The percentages here are invented for illustration.

Check yourself: before looking at the bars, what word would you have guessed? If it always picked the single most likely word, what might go wrong?

Step 4 · Attention · Part 3

Attention lets each word look at its context

What does “it” refer to?

Theanimaldidn’tcrossthestreetbecauseitwastootired.
Theanimaldidn’tcrossthestreetbecauseitwastoowide.

Dark box = where “it” pays the most attention. Light box = some attention.

Change one word and the meaning of “it” shifts. This mechanism is the heart of the transformer (2017), the design behind today’s chatbots.

In plain words

Words get their meaning from their context. In “it was too tired”, “it” means the animal; in “it was too wide”, “it” means the street. Attention is the mechanism that lets every word look at every other word and decide which ones matter for understanding it.

Stacking many layers of attention, with the neurons and weights from Part 2, gives the transformer (2017), the design behind modern chatbots. The highlighting here is a simplified illustration.

In plain words

Training the next-token guesser on a huge slice of the internet, books and code is called pretraining. It takes trillions of tokens and thousands of specialised chips running for weeks or months.

Nobody programs facts in. Grammar, facts and patterns of reasoning emerge because they all help predict the next token. The sizes shown come from published papers; the newest models are bigger and usually not published.

Sources: Brown et al., 2020 (GPT-3); Meta AI, 2024 (Llama 3)

Becoming an assistant · Part 3

Three stages turn a text predictor into a chatbot

Stage 1

Pretraining

Predict the next token on huge amounts of text. It knows language, but only continues text.

▶
Stage 2

Fine-tuning

Keep training on example conversations, so it learns to answer and follow instructions.

▶
Stage 3

Feedback

People (and other models) rate answers; the model is nudged toward helpful, honest ones.

In plain words

A pretrained model only continues text. Ask it “What is the capital of France?” and it might reply with another quiz question, because lists of quiz questions are common online.

Fine-tuning on example conversations teaches it to answer. Then feedback (people comparing pairs of answers; often called RLHF) nudges it toward answers that are helpful and honest. This is also where companies shape the tone and safety behaviour of the assistant.

Beyond text · Part 3

The same recipe now sees, hears and acts

Seeing

Images are cut into small patches, and each patch becomes a token.

Hearing

Speech is turned into tokens too, so models can listen and talk.

Acting

It writes a request to a tool (search, run code) and reads the result back.

Underneath: turn it into tokens, predict what comes next.

In plain words

The same recipe works for other kinds of data: images are cut into patches and sound into short slices, and each becomes tokens. Models can also be given tools: the model writes a request such as “run this Python code”, software outside the model carries it out, and the result is pasted back in as more tokens.

That is how a chatbot can analyse a spreadsheet like the one from Workshop 1: it writes and runs code, rather than doing the arithmetic in its head.

Limits · Part 4

Four ways it goes wrong

Hallucination

Confident, fluent and false, like a citation to a paper that doesn’t exist.

Bias

It learns the patterns in its data, including the unfair ones.

Knowledge cutoff

It knows nothing after its training data ends, unless a tool looks it up.

No built-in fact check

Training rewards plausible text. Plausible and true usually overlap, but not always.

In plain words

All four problems follow from how the model works. It always produces the most plausible continuation, so a made-up citation can look as convincing as a real one (hallucination). It copies the patterns in its data, including unfair ones (bias). Its weights freeze when training ends (cutoff). And nothing in training rewards “true”, only “plausible”.

Ground rules for the lab: check citations, statistics and methods details against the original source; test any AI-written code on made-up data and check the output by hand; and never paste participant data into a public chatbot.

The one thing to remember

Fluent isn’t the same as true.

Treat an AI answer as a draft to check, not a source to cite.

In plain words

If you remember only one thing from this guide, make it this. These systems are extraordinarily good at producing text that sounds right, because that is exactly what they were trained to do. Sounding right and being right usually go together, which is why they are so useful, but the model has no separate step that checks the truth.

Checking is your job.
