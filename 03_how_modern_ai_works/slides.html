    The big picture

    AI is a family of nested ideas

    Artificial intelligence
    Machine learning
    Deep learning
    LLMs

    Artificial intelligence

    Machines doing tasks we'd say need intelligence.

    Machine learning

    Learning patterns from examples, not hand-written rules.

    Deep learning

    Machine learning with large, many-layered neural networks.

    Large language models (LLMs)

    Deep networks trained on vast amounts of text.

    Presenter notes

    “AI” is a moving target: chess programs were AI in the 1990s; now we barely call them that. Most of what people mean by AI today sits in the inner boxes. Chatbots like ChatGPT, Claude and Gemini are large language models, the innermost box. The rest of the session zooms into it.

    The key shift

    Machine learning flips programming around

    Traditional programming

    Rules + data → answers

    A person writes the rules: “If the email says free money, mark it as spam.”

    Machine learning

    Data + answers → rules

    Show it thousands of emails already labeled spam or not, and let it work out the patterns itself.

    Presenter notes

    The single most important idea of the session. For decades, programmers tried to write rules for things like recognizing faces or translating languages, and it mostly failed: the world has too many exceptions. Machine learning learns the rules from examples instead. The catch: the “rules” it learns are millions of numbers, not sentences anyone can read.

    The building block

    An artificial neuron is a tiny calculator

    1 · Inputs

    Furry: 1
    Barks: 1
    Wings: 0

    2 · Weights

    × 0.6
    × 0.9
    × −1.0

    ▶
    3 · Add up
    1.5

    0.6+0.9+0

    ▶
    4 · Squash
    0.82

    “Probably a dog”

    Stack millions or billions of these in layers and you have a neural network. Nobody sets the weights by hand: they are learned.

    Presenter notes

    Walk through the numbers. Each input is multiplied by a weight that says how much it matters: barking matters a lot (0.9), wings count against “dog” (−1.0). Add them up: 1.5. Squash into 0–1 (the sigmoid function): 0.82, “82% confident it's a dog”. Ask: why is the wings weight negative?

    Brain-lab hook: inputs ≈ dendrites, weights ≈ synaptic strength, output ≈ firing. It's a cartoon of a real neuron; we come back to that in the discussion.

    Training

    Learning means nudging the weights downhill

    Height = how wrong the model is. Each square is one training step.

    1. Guess. Run an example through the network.
    2. Measure the error. Compare the guess with the right answer.
    3. Nudge every weight a little in the direction that shrinks the error.

    Repeat, billions of times.

    Presenter notes

    Analogy: you're on a foggy hillside and want to reach the valley floor. You can't see the bottom, but you can feel which way the ground slopes under your feet, so you take a small step downhill, and repeat. That's gradient descent. The slope is computed by backpropagation, which works out how much each weight contributed to the error. Real networks have billions of weights, so the hillside has billions of dimensions, but the idea is the same.

    Step 1 · Tokens

    First, text is chopped into tokens

    “Students love unbelievably long lectures.”

    Studentsloveunbelievablylonglectures.

    ▼

    [ 9218, 812, 3021, 403, 7745, 1102, 1588, 6431, 984, 13 ]

    The model never sees letters, only a list of numbers. One token is roughly ¾ of an English word. (Split and IDs are illustrative.)

    Presenter notes

    Now we zoom into a language model, in four steps. Common words get their own token; rarer words are split into reusable pieces. Each piece has an ID in a fixed vocabulary, typically tens of thousands to a couple of hundred thousand entries. The split and IDs here are made up; every model has its own tokenizer. Fun consequence: models have historically struggled to count the letters in a word, because they never see letters.

    Step 2 · Embeddings

    Each token becomes a point in “meaning space”

    Words used in similar ways land close together.

    Directions carry meaning: king → queen is the same step as man → woman.

    Real models use thousands of dimensions. We can only draw two.

    Presenter notes

    Each token ID is looked up in a giant table and turned into a long list of numbers, its embedding: think of it as coordinates. During training, tokens used in similar contexts get pushed to similar places, so meaning becomes geometry. The classic word2vec finding (2013): king − man + woman lands near queen. This 2D map is a simplified drawing, not real model data.

    Step 3 · Prediction

    The model's one job: guess the next token

    “The students opened their ______”

    books
    41% laptops
    28% notes
    12% minds
    4%

    Pick one, add it to the text, and predict again. Every reply is written one token at a time. (Illustrative probabilities.)

    Presenter notes

    Be the model: before showing the bars (start in reference view or cover them), read “The students opened their…” and have the room shout the next word. You just did what the model does, and you produced a spread of answers, not one.

    The network outputs a probability for every token in its vocabulary; one is chosen (usually a likely one, with a little randomness called temperature), appended, and the whole thing runs again. Ask: what goes wrong if it always picks the single top word? (Repetitive, bland text.) The percentages are invented.

    Step 4 · Attention

    Attention lets each word look at its context

    What does “it” refer to?

    Theanimaldidn'tcrossthestreetbecauseitwastootired.
    Theanimaldidn'tcrossthestreetbecauseitwastoowide.

    Solid = where “it” pays the most attention. Faint = some attention.

    Change one word and the meaning of “it” shifts. This mechanism is the heart of the transformer (2017), the design behind today's chatbots.

    Presenter notes

    Before 2017, language models read text one word at a time and struggled to connect words far apart. The transformer (“Attention Is All You Need”) lets every token look at every other token and decide how relevant each one is. Here, one word at the end (tired vs. wide) changes what “it” attends to. Real models run many attention “heads” in parallel across dozens of layers. The highlighting is a simplified illustration.

    Pretraining

    Pretraining repeats that guess at enormous scale

    175 billion

    weights in GPT-3, released in 2020

    15 trillion

    tokens of text used to train Meta's Llama 3 (2024)

    Thousands

    of specialized chips, running for weeks to months

    Newer models are bigger still, but most companies no longer publish exact sizes.

    Sources: Brown et al., 2020 (GPT-3); Meta AI, 2024 (Llama 3)

    Presenter notes

    Pretraining is the next-token game from step 3, played on a huge slice of the internet, books and code. Every wrong guess nudges the weights a little (the downhill walk). Over trillions of tokens the model absorbs grammar, facts and styles of reasoning, because all of those help predict the next word. Nobody programs in facts: they emerge as a side effect of prediction.

    Becoming an assistant

    Three stages turn a text predictor into a chatbot

    Stage 1

    Pretraining

    Predict the next token on huge amounts of text. It knows language, but only continues text.

    ▶
    Stage 2

    Fine-tuning

    Keep training on example conversations, so it learns to answer and follow instructions.

    ▶
    Stage 3

    Feedback

    People (and other models) rate answers; the model is nudged toward helpful, honest ones.

    Presenter notes

    A pretrained model asked “What is the capital of France?” might just continue with “What is the capital of Germany?”, because lists of quiz questions are common online. Fine-tuning on example dialogues teaches the question-and-answer format. Stage 3 is often called RLHF, reinforcement learning from human feedback: people compare pairs of answers and the model is trained toward the kind they prefer. This is also where tone, safety behaviour and refusals get shaped.

    Beyond text

    The same recipe now sees, hears and acts

    01 · Seeing

    Images are cut into small patches, and each patch becomes a token.

    02 · Hearing

    Speech is turned into tokens too, so models can listen and talk.

    03 · Acting

    It writes a request to a tool (search, run code) and reads the result back.

    Underneath: turn it into tokens, predict what comes next.

    Presenter notes

    Modern assistants are “multimodal”: one model handles text, images and audio by converting each into tokens. “Agents” go further: the model outputs a message like “run this Python code”, software outside the model carries it out and pastes the result back in as more tokens. That's how a chatbot can analyse a CSV like Workshop 1's: it writes and runs code rather than doing the arithmetic in its head.

    Limits

    Four ways it goes wrong

    Hallucination

    Confident, fluent and false, like a citation to a paper that doesn't exist.

    Bias

    It learns the patterns in its data, including the unfair ones.

    Knowledge cutoff

    It knows nothing after its training data ends, unless a tool looks it up.

    No built-in fact check

    Training rewards plausible text. Plausible and true usually overlap, but not always.

    Presenter notes

    All four follow from the mechanism. Hallucination: the model always produces the most plausible continuation, and a made-up reference can look as plausible as a real one. Bias: it mirrors its data. Cutoff: the weights freeze when training ends.

    Lab ground rules: check citations, statistics and methods details against the original source; test AI-written code on mock data; never paste participant data into a public chatbot.

    The one thing to remember

    Fluent isn't the same as true.

    Treat an AI answer as a draft to check, not a source to cite.

    Presenter notes

    Pause here. These systems are extraordinarily good at producing text that sounds right, because that is exactly what they were trained to do. Sounding right and being right usually go together, which is why they're useful, but the model has no separate step that checks the truth. That's your job.
