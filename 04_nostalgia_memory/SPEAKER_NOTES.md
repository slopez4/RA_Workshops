## 4 · What nostalgia is (1:00)
Definition: social, self-relevant, bittersweet but mostly positive (Hepper et al., 2024; Sedikides et al., 2008). History: a 17th-century medical diagnosis for homesickness. Lay conceptions: memories, relationships and happiness are central; daydreaming, regret and loneliness peripheral (Hepper et al., 2012), across 18 countries (Hepper et al., 2014).

## 5 · Anatomy of a nostalgic memory (1:00)
Content: self as protagonist, close others, momentous events, redemption (Wildschut et al., 2006). Appraisals: pleasant, irretrievable loss, distant, unique, shared only with longing (van Tilburg et al., 2019). Feeling: across 41 experiments (N = 4,659) it raised positive and ambivalent affect but did not significantly change negative affect, and the effect depended on the induction and control (Leunissen et al., 2021). The one-liner: "bittersweet is real; how bitter depends on how you measure it."

## 6 · Regulatory model (1:15)
Triggers on the left (negative mood, loneliness, meaning threat, cold, limited time, sensory stimuli), functions on the right (connectedness, self-continuity, meaning, approach). Wildschut and Sedikides (2023a). "A thermostat for psychological comfort." The senses are on the trigger side. Ask: which side is Zhou et al.'s (2012) cold-and-warmth finding on? (Both: cold triggers, nostalgia raises felt warmth.)

## 7 · Reality check (1:00 + Discuss A, 3:00)
Three numbers: g ≈ 0.39 across 347 experiments (Weingarten et al., 2026; **I only saw the repository abstract: say so**); daily life is negative for well-being while induced is positive (Newman et al., 2020); 28 countries plus Hong Kong (Hepper et al., 2024).
**Discuss A.** "What would you want to know before using an event reflection task in our lab?" Good answers: what is the control, demand characteristics, would a nostalgic event be an unusually positive memory chosen on request, sample, manipulation check.

## 8 · Memory machinery (1:15)
Walk the four steps: cue → match (encoding specificity, Tulving & Thomson, 1973) → construction (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000) → sensory reactivation (Wheeler et al., 2000). Involuntary retrieval (Berntsen, 1996); music memories used less executive involvement (El Haj et al., 2012). The hypothesis: a sensory cue is a fast, low-effort key into a trace that contains sensory details.

## 9 · Reminiscence bump (1:20)
The bump: about 10–30 for important events, about 5–30 for word cues (Munawar et al., 2018, 68 studies). Odor-cued memories came mostly from the first decade, word/picture cues peaked at 11–20 (Willander & Larsson, 2006, older adults); songs' salience peaked around 14 (Jakubowski et al., 2020, N = 470); cascading bumps from parents' music (Krumhansl & Zupnick, 2013). Stress the footnote: these ranges are from different studies and designs, not one experiment. Open question: is *nostalgia* bump-shaped? **Go back to slide 2:** where should smell dots sit?

## 10 · Feeling vs accuracy (1:00 + Discuss B, 3:00)
Leboe and Ansons (2006): nostalgia may partly be successful, fluent remembering misattributed to a pleasant past (word-list experiments). Rogers (2020, N = 409): the positive-affect advantage held for first-person, not third-person, recall. Gap: in our search we did not find studies checking nostalgic memories against an external record.
**Discuss B.** "How would you show a nostalgic memory is richer rather than rosier?" Good answers: blind-coded Autobiographical Interview details, word-count control, external verification (diary, dated photos), lab-staged event recalled in a nostalgic vs ordinary mode.

## 11 · Measuring memory (0:30, skim)
Autobiographical Interview (Levine et al., 2002; used by Belfi et al., 2016); ratings; age; LIWC; rehearsal. "Ratings alone cannot separate feeling from content."

## 12 · Which senses (0:40)
Sensory stimuli as a major trigger (Hepper et al., 2024); open-ended cues (Zaragoza Scherman et al., 2026, N = 256). The table is the map for the next four slides. Note the imbalance across senses.

## 13 · Smell I: LOVER (1:30)
Go letter by letter: Limbic (Herz et al., 2004, N = 5, a pilot), Old (Willander & Larsson, 2006, N = 93), Vivid (de Bruijn & Bender, 2018, N = 170 — but not Herz, 2004), Emotional (Herz & Schooler, 2002; Herz, 2004), Rare (Rubin et al., 1984). LOVER comes from Larsson et al. (2014). "It is a summary, not a law; V is the shakiest."

## 14 · Smell II: contested + scent-evoked nostalgia (1:00)
Naming the odor attenuates the childhood peak (Willander & Larsson, 2007); odor beat auditory but not visual triggers for an aversive film (Toffolo et al., 2012); no vividness or specificity difference in Herz (2004). Reid et al. (2015): arousal, familiarity and autobiographical relevance drive scent-evoked nostalgia; no other sense in the design. Hackländer et al. (2019): move from establishing to explaining.

## 15 · Music (1:30)
~30% of excerpts evoked an autobiographical memory, nostalgia third most common emotion (Janata et al., 2007). Barrett et al. (2010), conceptually replicated with self-selected songs and matched familiar controls (Hennessy et al., 2024, N = 582). Diaries: ~1/day, vivid, often involuntary (Jakubowski & Ghosh, 2021); music > food in frequency, involuntariness, importance (Jakubowski et al., 2023). Involuntariness alone doesn't explain music's richness (Belfi et al., 2022).

## 16 · Taste, temperature, vision (1:15)
Food: strong elicitor, predominantly positive, comfort via social connectedness (Reid et al., 2023, 2026). Temperature: cold triggers nostalgia, nostalgia raises felt warmth and cold tolerance (Zhou et al., 2012). Vision/immersion: Oba et al. (2016); VR (Yılmaz-Özdemir et al., 2025); the EEG study (Hu et al., 2025) is worth reading critically (the text I retrieved suggests a fixed order and non-matched controls — check before saying so).

## 17 · Minefield (0:40 + Discuss C, 3:00)
Eight confounds. **Discuss C.** "Pick two senses; how would you make a fair comparison?" Good answers: personally selected cues matched on familiarity and relevance; within-subject Latin square; odor-only vs named; same DVs; measure ability (smell test).

## 18 · Brain (0:50)
Yang et al. (2022): four components. Studies: Janata (2009), Oba et al. (2016), Herz et al. (2004; N = 5), Arshamian et al. (2013), Hennessy et al. (2025; N = 57). All small, different cues: a model to test, not a result.

## 19 · Aging and clinical (0:50)
Nostalgia in dementia (Ismail et al., 2018, three experiments, N = 27/29/50). Odor and music cues in Alzheimer's (El Haj et al., 2012, 2018); odor effect only in AD, not controls (Glachet & El Haj, 2019). Older adults recall older, less specific, more positive memories (Jakubowski et al., 2023). Why olfactory ability must be measured in older adults.

## Likely questions

- **"Is nostalgia good for you?"** On average in lab experiments, modestly (g ≈ 0.39 per the 2026 meta-analysis, to be verified); in daily life the picture is mixed or negative (Newman et al., 2020). Depends on induction and control.
- **"Does smell beat music?"** No study I found compares them head-to-head with matched, self-selected cues and nostalgia as the outcome. That is Seed 1.
- **"Is the Proust effect real?"** Parts replicate (emotional, older, rarely rehearsed); vividness is mixed; naming the odor changes it; most samples are older adults. Hackländer et al. (2019) say the field should now explain causes.
- **"Why didn't you cite X?"** It is a curated list, not a systematic review. The workbook has a search-log module for exactly that.
- **"Can I trust the numbers?"** They come from abstracts. Open the paper before you quote.
