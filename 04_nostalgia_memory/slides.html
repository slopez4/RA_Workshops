1970sPart 1 · What nostalgia is

A bittersweet emotion about the personal past

Working definition

A social, self-relevant, bittersweet (although mostly positive) emotion that arises when reflecting on fond past memories.

Hepper et al., 2024; Sedikides et al., 2008

From disease to emotion
  • 1600s: a medical diagnosis (Greek nostos, return home + algos, pain)
  • Later: a psychiatric disorder
  • 2000s: a mostly positive, self-relevant, social emotion with key psychological functions

Sedikides et al., 2008

What lay people put at the center

Central: memories, relationships, happiness
Peripheral: daydreaming, regret, loneliness

Hepper et al., 2012

Is it universal?

The same structure appeared in 18 countries (N = 1,704): nostalgia looks pancultural.

Hepper et al., 2014

In plain words

The word began as a 17th-century medical diagnosis for homesickness. Modern social psychologists reframed it as an ordinary emotion: wistful, a bit sad because the past is gone, but mostly warm and about the people who matter to you.

To find out what people mean by nostalgia, Hepper and colleagues asked lay people to list and rate its features. Memories, relationships and happiness were central; daydreaming, regret and loneliness were peripheral. Students in 18 countries agreed on the ranking, which is why researchers call it pancultural.

Check yourself: how is nostalgia different from just remembering something fondly? (Slide 5 gives a start.)

1970sPart 1 · What nostalgia is

Anatomy of a nostalgic memory

Content
  • The self as protagonist
  • Close others (friends, family) or momentous events (e.g., weddings)
  • More positive than negative affect
  • Redemption: bad scenes followed by triumphs

Wildschut et al., 2006

Appraisals
  • Pleasant
  • Involves an irretrievable loss
  • Feels psychologically distant
  • Feels unique

This profile differs from all other emotions tested, sharing only longing (van Tilburg et al., 2019)

Feeling

Across 41 experiments (N = 4,659), nostalgia inductions raised positive and ambivalent affect, but did not significantly change negative affect.

The size of the effect varied markedly with the induction and the control condition.

Leunissen et al., 2021

“Bittersweet” is real, but how bitter depends on how you elicit and measure it.

In plain words

Three descriptions of the same thing. Content: when people write about nostalgic experiences, they are the main character, with people they love, in an important moment. Often something bad turns out well. Appraisals: nostalgic events are good but gone for good, far away in time, and one of a kind. Feeling: mostly positive, with some mixed feelings.

The last point matters for design. Leunissen and colleagues pooled 41 experiments. Whether nostalgia looks purely happy or bittersweet depends on what you compare it with (an ordinary memory? a positive one?) and how you induced it.

Check yourself: which of the three (content, appraisals, feeling) would you code from written memories, and which would you ask people to rate?

W06 Wildschut et al., 2006 · Z08 Zhou et al., 2008 · R11 Routledge et al., 2011 · S14 Stephan et al., 2014 · Z12 Zhou et al., 2012 · H21 Hepper et al., 2021 · H24 Hepper et al., 2024 · S16 Sedikides et al., 2016

In plain words

A lot of experiments share one logic: put people in a bad or threatened state, and they become more nostalgic; make them nostalgic, and they feel more connected, more continuous with their past self, more that life is meaningful, and more ready to act. Wildschut and Sedikides (2023a) organize this as a regulatory model, like a thermostat for psychological comfort.

It is a model, not a law. It makes testable predictions (for example, causal mediation tests) and has boundary conditions.

Check yourself: Zhou et al. (2012) found that coldness triggered nostalgia and nostalgia raised felt warmth. Which side of the model is each finding on?

Discuss · 3 min

Lab benefits are small to moderate, and daily-life nostalgia looks different. What would you want to know before using an event reflection task in our lab?

In plain words

A Hedges’ g of about 0.39 is a small-to-moderate effect: you need roughly 100 people per group to detect it reliably in a simple two-group experiment (the workbook has a power sandbox). The meta-analysis is from 2026 and I could only read its repository abstract, so verify the numbers in the paper.

Newman et al. (2020) argue that the typical lab procedure (“recall a nostalgic event”) generates relatively positive memories on request, whereas in everyday life nostalgia is more mixed or even negative. That does not make the lab findings wrong; it limits how far they generalize.

Discussion prompts: What does the control condition do? What does an “ordinary event” control equate and what does it not? How would you check demand characteristics?

1980sPart 2 · The memory lens

Nostalgia is an autobiographical memory, retrieved by a cue

1

A cue appears — a smell, a song, a photo. Retrieval is often involuntary (Berntsen, 1996).

2

It matches the trace — a cue works to the extent it overlaps with what was encoded: encoding specificity (Tulving & Thomson, 1973).

3

The memory is constructed — from lifetime periods, general events and event details, guided by the self (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000).

4

Senses reactivate — vivid remembering re-engages sensory-specific cortex (Wheeler et al., 2000).

Why this matters for sensory nostalgia
  • A sensory cue is literally part of the original event, so it should be a good key.
  • Cues can bring memories with less effortful search: music memories engaged fewer executive processes (El Haj et al., 2012).
  • The kind of memory a cue opens may depend on when the sense was most tied to experience.
In plain words

Autobiographical memories are rebuilt each time from pieces. A cue that overlaps with what you encoded at the time is a good retrieval key (encoding specificity). Many cue-triggered memories pop up without a deliberate search (involuntary memories). And when you vividly remember a picture or a sound, the parts of your brain that process pictures or sounds become active again.

Put those together and you get a simple hypothesis for sensory nostalgia: a smell or a song gives a fast, low-effort route into a trace that contains sensory details. The research below tests where that holds and where it does not.

Check yourself: why might the same cue (a smell) bring back a memory from a different life period than a word describing that smell?

Why a bump?

Identity formation and cultural life scripts have the most support (Rathbone et al., 2008; Munawar et al., 2018).

Music

Also a cascading bump: liking and memories rise for music from your parents’ youth (Krumhansl & Zupnick, 2013).

Open question

Is nostalgia itself bump-shaped, and does the peak depend on the cue’s sense?

In plain words

The reminiscence bump is the tendency for older adults to recall disproportionately many memories from roughly ages 10 to 30. A systematic review of 68 studies (Munawar et al., 2018) puts the window at about 10–30 for important events and about 5–30 for word cues. The leading explanations tie it to identity formation (we remember well the years when our self took shape).

Cue type changes the picture. In older adults, odor-cued memories came mostly from the first decade, whereas word and picture cues peaked at 11–20 (Willander & Larsson, 2006). For songs, autobiographical salience peaked around age 14 (Jakubowski et al., 2020, N = 470).

The bars above are a visual summary of ranges reported in different studies. They are not data from one experiment.

1980sPart 2 · The memory lens

A feeling about remembering, or a record of the past?

Fluency misattribution

When some words were encoded to support richer recollection, people judged having met them in a more positive context. Nostalgia may partly be successful remembering misattributed to a pleasant past.

Leboe & Ansons, 2006

Perspective matters

Nostalgic (vs ordinary) recall raised positive affect when recalled from a first-person view, but not from a third-person view.

Rogers, 2020 (N = 409)

A gap for a memory lab

In our (non-systematic) search we did not find studies that check nostalgic memories against an external record of what happened.

Discuss · 3 min

How would you show that a nostalgic memory is richer rather than just rosier? What ground truth could we get in our lab?

In plain words

Leboe and Ansons (2006) proposed that the warm feeling of nostalgia can come from how easily and richly we remember, which we mistakenly credit to the past having been good. Their experiments used word lists, not personal memories, so they show the mechanism is possible, not that it explains all nostalgia.

Rogers (2020) adds that perspective (seeing the memory through your own eyes or from outside) changes how much positive affect nostalgic recall carries.

What is missing is a direct test of accuracy: for example, a lab-staged event or a documented life event, remembered later in a nostalgic or ordinary mode and scored against the record.

Check yourself: why is “more detailed” not the same as “more accurate”?

Pick measures that can separate feeling from content: ratings alone cannot.

In plain words

The Autobiographical Interview (Levine et al., 2002) is the memory field’s standard way to quantify how episodic a recall is. Trained coders who do not know the condition count details that refer to the event (internal) and those that do not (external). Belfi et al. (2016) used it to show music-evoked memories had a greater proportion of internal details and more perceptual details than memories evoked by famous faces.

Rating scales are fast but mix up how a memory feels with what it contains. If you only measure the feeling, you cannot tell “richer” from “rosier”.

1990sPart 3 · Smell

The Proust effect: odor memories are LOVER

L

Limbic

Personally meaningful odors drove larger amygdala and hippocampal responses (N = 5, pilot).

Herz et al., 2004
O

Old

Most odor-cued memories came from the first decade; word/picture cues peaked at 11–20.

Willander & Larsson, 2006 (N = 93)
V

Vivid

Childhood odors gave richer memories than images (N = 170). But no vividness difference in Herz (2004).

de Bruijn & Bender, 2018
E

Emotional

The same cue was more emotional as an odor than seen or heard.

Herz & Schooler, 2002; Herz, 2004
R

Rare

Odor memories had been thought and talked about less beforehand.

Rubin et al., 1984

LOVER is a summary of the pattern, not a law: V is the shakiest letter.

LOVER acronym: Larsson et al., 2014 · review: Hackländer et al., 2019

In plain words

Proust’s madeleine gave the field its name: the idea that smells (and tastes) unlock vivid, emotional, long-forgotten childhood memories. Experiments from 1984 on have supported parts of this. Larsson and colleagues (2014) summarized the behavioral and neural findings as LOVER: Limbic, Old, Vivid, Emotional, Rare.

Look at each letter’s evidence base. “Limbic” rests on a five-person fMRI pilot plus a later fMRI study; “Old” on older-adult samples; “Vivid” is mixed (Herz, 2004 found no vividness or specificity difference; de Bruijn & Bender, 2018 and Chu & Downes, 2002 found richer or more detailed memories). Hackländer et al. (2019) argue the field should now explain why.

Check yourself: which letter would you test first, and what comparison cue would you use?

1990sPart 3 · Smell

What is contested — and scent-evoked nostalgia

Contested or conditional
  • Naming the odor reduced the childhood peak and pleasantness (N = 72) Willander & Larsson, 2007
  • Aversive film, matched triggers (N = 70): odor beat auditory, not visual triggers on detail and arousal Toffolo et al., 2012
  • Vividness and specificity did not differ by cue form Herz, 2004
  • Most evidence is from older adults, with different cue sets per modality
  • Field advice: move from establishing to explaining Hackländer et al., 2019
Scent-evoked nostalgia

Participants sampled 12 scents. Nostalgia was greater for scents that were arousing, familiar and autobiographically relevant, and in people high in nostalgia proneness.

It predicted positive affect, self-esteem, self-continuity, optimism, social connectedness and meaning, and carried more positive emotion than non-nostalgic memories.

Reid et al., 2015

Back to slide 2: does smell sit left of the other cues in our chart?

In plain words

The odor literature is a good case study in how an exciting effect gets complicated. Telling people what the smell is changes the memories (Willander & Larsson, 2007), so “odor vs word” is not a clean contrast. Odor beat sound but not visual cues for memories of an aversive film (Toffolo et al., 2012). Herz (2004) found odors more emotional but not more vivid or specific.

Reid et al. (2015) is the most direct nostalgia study: the same three ingredients that drive music nostalgia (arousal, familiarity, autobiographical relevance) also drive scent nostalgia. It measures scent-by-scent ratings and has no other sense in the design.

Check yourself: why does it matter that a scent’s familiarity predicts nostalgia when we try to compare smell with music?

1990sPart 3 · Music

Music: frequent, bittersweet, often involuntary

Janata et al., 2007

Jakubowski & Ghosh, 2021

music > food

more frequent, more involuntary, more important memories (39 young + 39 older)

Jakubowski et al., 2023

What predicts it

Nostalgia grows with autobiographical salience, arousal, familiarity and mixed emotions; nostalgia proneness adds to it (Barrett et al., 2010).

Why so rich?

Music memories beat face memories on detail (Belfi et al., 2016), and involuntariness alone doesn’t explain it (Belfi et al., 2022).

In plain words

Music is the best-studied sensory route to nostalgia. Hearing a song from your past often brings a memory (about 30% of excerpts in one corpus-based study) and nostalgia is among the most common emotions it evokes. The original finding (Barrett et al., 2010) that nostalgia grows with a song’s autobiographical salience, arousal and familiarity was conceptually replicated in a larger sample with self-selected songs and musically matched controls (Hennessy et al., 2024).

Diary studies show how this plays out in daily life: music memories are frequent and often involuntary (Jakubowski & Ghosh, 2021) and, compared with food, produce more memories, more involuntary ones, and more personally important ones (Jakubowski et al., 2023).

Why music memories are so rich is still open: it is not just that they are involuntary (Belfi et al., 2022).

Check yourself: what is the advantage of the matched, familiar, non-nostalgic songs in Hennessy et al. (2024)?

1990sPart 3 · Taste, touch, sight

Beyond smell and sound

Taste & food
  • A powerful elicitor; profile like other elicitors but predominantly positive (3 studies incl. tasting 12 flavours) Reid et al., 2023
  • Nostalgia for food raised comfort via social connectedness (preregistered) Reid et al., 2026
  • Six themes, from childhood to homesickness Vignolles & Pichon, 2014
Temperature & touch
  • Cold days and rooms raised nostalgia
  • Nostalgia raised perceived warmth
  • Nostalgic recall increased tolerance of noxious cold

Zhou et al., 2012

Vision, immersion, multisensory
  • Childhood images engaged hippocampus + reward regions Oba et al., 2016
  • A VR task gave stronger, longer-lasting felt nostalgia than the standard task Yılmaz-Özdemir et al., 2025
  • EEG: auditory and audiovisual > visual state nostalgia Hu et al., 2025

Food and temperature link nostalgia to body states and belonging: a different mechanism from vivid recall?

In plain words

Food is a strong elicitor of nostalgia and mostly a positive, social one: foods tied to family and occasions bring togetherness, which the preregistered experiments link to comfort (Reid et al., 2023, 2026). Troisi and Gabriel (2011) show that comfort foods activate relationship concepts, which fits.

Temperature is the clearest case of the body and nostalgia talking to each other (Zhou et al., 2012): coldness raises nostalgia, nostalgia raises felt warmth. This is a nice replication target.

Vision and immersion: Oba et al. (2016) is the key fMRI study with visual cues; VR is a new induction method. The EEG study (Hu et al., 2025) is worth reading critically: the text I retrieved suggests a fixed presentation order and non-matched control stimuli, so check it yourself.

Comparing senses is a minefield

Familiarity & relevance

Both drive nostalgia, so unequal cues confound the senses.
Barrett 2010; Reid 2015

Experimenter-chosen vs self-selected, with matched controls.
Hennessy 2024

Labels

Matching

Intensity, valence, arousal, salience equated across modalities.
Toffolo 2012

Order

A fixed order confounds modality with time.

Sample

Glachet 2019

Outcome

Herz 2004; de Bruijn 2018

Discuss · 3 min

Pick two senses. How would you make a fair comparison of how nostalgic they make people? What would you hold constant, and what would you give up?

In plain words

When one study says “smell beats vision” and another says “music beats food,” ask what else differed. Most apparent differences between senses can be produced by differences in how familiar the cues were, how they were chosen, whether they were named, the order of testing, and who was tested.

The workbook has a “Spot the confound” exercise built from exactly these issues.

Discussion prompts: Is it possible to match a smell, a song and a picture on everything except sense? What would a Latin-square order look like with three senses?

1990sPart 3 · Brain

A proposed neural model of nostalgia

1Self-reflection

mPFC · PCC · precuneus. Dorsal mPFC tracked how autobiographically salient each song was (Janata, 2009).

3Reward

striatum · SN/VTA · vmPFC. Hippocampus–ventral striatum coupling scaled with nostalgia tendency (Oba et al., 2016).

2Autobiographical memory

hippocampus + midline. Odor memories: amygdala and hippocampus (Herz et al., 2004, N = 5); medial temporal and emotion regions (Arshamian et al., 2013).

4Emotion regulation

ACC · mPFC. Nostalgic music: default-mode, salience, reward, medial temporal networks; stronger in older adults (Hennessy et al., 2025, N = 57).

Model: Yang et al., 2022 (prospective review). The authors note only a handful of neural studies existed; the studies shown are small and use different cues. Head drawing is schematic.

In plain words

Yang and colleagues (2022) propose that nostalgia recruits four sets of processes: self-reflection, autobiographical memory, emotion regulation and reward, each with candidate brain regions. It is a model to test, not a result.

The empirical studies fit loosely: music-evoked salience in dorsal medial prefrontal cortex (Janata, 2009), hippocampus–striatum coupling for visual childhood cues (Oba et al., 2016), amygdala/hippocampus for a personal odor (Herz et al., 2004, five participants), and network-level default-mode, salience and reward engagement for personalized nostalgic music, stronger in older adults (Hennessy et al., 2025).

Check yourself: why is it hard to compare these studies with each other? (Think cue type, sample, control stimuli.)

1990sPart 3 · Aging & clinical

Nostalgia and sensory cues across the lifespan

Dementia
  • Three experiments (N = 27, 29, 50): nostalgia raised connectedness, meaning, self-continuity, optimism, self-esteem, positive affect; nostalgic participants recalled more self-referent dementia information Ismail et al., 2018
  • Systematic review & meta-analysis of experimental evidence Ismail et al., 2020
Cues in Alzheimer’s disease
  • Odor and music improved specificity, emotion, retrieval time in mild AD El Haj et al., 2018
  • Odor raised arousal, reliving and positivity only in AD, not in healthy controls Glachet & El Haj, 2019
Healthy aging

Older adults recalled older, less specific, more positive, vivid and rehearsed music and food memories (Jakubowski et al., 2023) and showed stronger responses to nostalgic music (Hennessy et al., 2025). Nostalgia proneness went with maintained well-being from 18 to 91, and buffered limited time horizons (Hepper et al., 2021).

In plain words

This is where the memory and nostalgia literatures meet clinically. Sensory cues can help when autobiographical memory is impaired: in mild Alzheimer’s disease, odor and music cues improved the specificity and emotion of recalled memories. In healthy older adults the odor benefit was not seen (Glachet & El Haj, 2019).

Nostalgia itself seems to be beneficial for people with dementia in small experiments (Ismail et al., 2018). Most of these clinical samples are small, so treat them as promising, not settled.

Check yourself: why must a study of odor cues in older adults measure olfactory ability?

NowPart 4 · Methods

The methods toolkit

NeedOptionsWatch out for
InduceEvent reflection task (nostalgic vs ordinary event); self-selected songs; scents; foods; VRVR beat the event reflection task on felt nostalgia (Yılmaz-Özdemir 2025); effects depend on the induction (Leunissen 2021)

In plain words

The most common induction is the event reflection task: participants recall and describe a nostalgic or an ordinary event, then complete measures. VR, songs, scents and foods are alternatives. Trait measures (Southampton Nostalgia Scale, Nostalgia Inventory) capture proneness; state items work as manipulation checks.

Choosing the control is a major design decision. Jiang et al. (2021) compared nostalgia with reflection and brooding; Hennessy et al. (2024, 2025) compared nostalgic songs with familiar non-nostalgic songs matched on features.

The workbook includes practice versions of state items and an event reflection task so you can experience the method; use validated instruments in any real study.

Key words

Nostalgia
A social, self-relevant, bittersweet (mostly positive) emotion about the personal past.
Nostalgia proneness
A trait: how readily and strongly someone feels nostalgia.
Event reflection task
Standard induction: recall a nostalgic (vs ordinary) event and describe it.
Autobiographical memory
Memory for personal events and facts about one’s life.
Encoding specificity
A cue works to the extent it matches what was encoded.
Involuntary memory
A memory that comes to mind without a deliberate search.
Reminiscence bump
Disproportionate recall of events from about ages 10–30.
Proust effect
The idea that odors evoke especially vivid, emotional, old memories.
MEAM
Music-evoked autobiographical memory.
Autobiographical Interview
Coding scheme for internal (episodic) vs external (semantic) details.
Self-continuity
A sense of connection between one’s past and present self.
Confound
A second difference between conditions that could explain the result.
