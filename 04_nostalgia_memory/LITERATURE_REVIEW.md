## 0. How to read this review (and how far to trust it)

**What it is.** A narrative, annotated overview built from web searches in October 2026, aimed at generating research questions. It is **not** a systematic review.

**How the facts were checked.**
- Every reference was looked up in Crossref by DOI, so authors, years, journals and pages come from the publisher's record.
- For almost every paper cited with a finding, I pulled the abstract (Europe PMC or Crossref) and wrote the claim from the abstract, not from memory. Where I could only see a summary, the text says so.
- Things I deliberately **left out** because I could not verify them: a statistic about the share of music excerpts that evoked nostalgia in Barrett et al. (2010) that came from a secondary source; a claimed failed replication of a nostalgia and error-related-negativity finding (secondary source only); a claim that scents are a stronger nostalgia inducer than songs (it is not in the abstract of the paper it was attributed to).
- **Weingarten et al. (2026)**, the new meta-analysis, is cited from its repository abstract. I could not open the full text, so check the numbers before you quote them.
- Everything about "what has not been done" means *I did not find it in these searches*. Part of your homework is to run your own, more careful search.

**One more caution.** Nostalgia is a field with one very productive group (Sedikides, Wildschut and colleagues, University of Southampton), so much of the experimental literature shares methods and assumptions. Read it critically, and read the dissenting and ecological work (Newman et al., 2020; Leunissen et al., 2021) alongside it.

## 1. Ten take-home points

1. **Nostalgia is a social, self-relevant, bittersweet but mostly positive emotion** about the personal past (Sedikides et al., 2008; Hepper et al., 2012, 2014).
2. **It is triggered by discomfort** (negative mood, loneliness, meaning threat, cold, limited time) and **appears to counter it** (more social connectedness, self-continuity, meaning, approach motivation). This is the regulatory model (Wildschut & Sedikides, 2023a).
3. **Lab benefits are modest on average and depend on the induction and control condition.** The first meta-analytic synthesis I found reports g ≈ 0.39 for benefits (Weingarten et al., 2026, per its abstract), and the hedonic profile varies by induction (Leunissen et al., 2021).
4. **Daily-life nostalgia looks different from induced nostalgia.** In experience-sampling work, state nostalgia related negatively to well-being (Newman et al., 2020).
5. **Nostalgia is an autobiographical-memory phenomenon**, so the memory literature (cue effectiveness, the reminiscence bump, involuntary retrieval, sensory reactivation) tells us what to expect from sensory cues.
6. **Part of nostalgia may be a feeling about remembering rather than a record of the past**: fluent, rich recollection can bias people toward a pleasant past (Leboe & Ansons, 2006), and the positive-affect boost depends on recall perspective (Rogers, 2020).
7. **Smell: older, emotional, rarely rehearsed, sometimes richer.** The "Proust effect" is replicated in parts and contested in others (Herz, 2004; Willander & Larsson, 2006; Toffolo et al., 2012; de Bruijn & Bender, 2018; Hackländer et al., 2019).
8. **Music: frequent, often involuntary, vivid and bittersweet.** Familiarity, arousal and autobiographical salience drive music-evoked nostalgia, and the main findings have been conceptually replicated (Barrett et al., 2010; Hennessy et al., 2024).
9. **Food, temperature and vision also trigger nostalgia**, but with less work: food is a strong elicitor (Reid et al., 2023), cold triggers nostalgia and nostalgia raises felt warmth (Zhou et al., 2012), and immersive VR strengthens felt nostalgia (Yılmaz-Özdemir et al., 2025).
10. **Comparisons across senses are confounded** by cue familiarity, personal relevance, labeling, design (within vs between), order and sample age. This is where the best new research questions are.

## 2. What is nostalgia?

### Definition and history
Nostalgia began as a medical diagnosis (Greek *nostos*, return home, plus *algos*, pain) and was long treated as a disease. Modern social psychology reframed it as "a predominantly positive, self-relevant, and social emotion serving key psychological functions" (Sedikides et al., 2008). A more recent definition: a social, self-relevant, bittersweet (although mostly positive) emotion that arises when reflecting on fond past memories (Hepper et al., 2024).

### What laypeople mean
Using a prototype approach, Hepper et al. (2012) found that lay people treat memories, relationships and happiness as *central* features of nostalgia and daydreaming, regret and loneliness as *peripheral*. The structure replicated across 18 countries (N = 1,704; Hepper et al., 2014), supporting the claim that nostalgia is pancultural.

### Content and appraisals
- **Content.** Across seven studies, nostalgic narratives featured the self as protagonist with close others or in momentous events (weddings, for example), contained more positive than negative affect, and often showed negative scenes redeemed by later triumphs (Wildschut et al., 2006).
- **Appraisals.** Nostalgia-eliciting events are pleasant, involve an irretrievable loss, feel psychologically distant, and are unique; the appraisal profile is shared only with longing (van Tilburg et al., 2019).
- **Hedonic tone.** In an integrative analysis of 41 experiments (N = 4,659), nostalgia inductions increased positive and ambivalent affect but did not significantly change negative affect, and effects varied markedly with the induction and the comparison condition (Leunissen et al., 2021). "Bittersweet" is thus real, but how bitter depends on how you measure it.

## 3. Triggers and functions: the regulatory model

**Triggers found in experiments.** Negative mood and loneliness (Wildschut et al., 2006; Zhou et al., 2008), threats to meaning (Routledge et al., 2011), avoidance motivation (Stephan et al., 2014), coldness (Zhou et al., 2012), and limited time horizons (Hepper et al., 2021). Sensory stimuli and social gatherings are major self-reported triggers (Hepper et al., 2024).

**Functions.** Nostalgia increases social connectedness, self-continuity, meaning in life, positive affect and self-esteem, and strengthens approach motivation and optimism (Wildschut et al., 2006; Routledge et al., 2011, 2012; Sedikides et al., 2016; Stephan et al., 2014; Abeyta et al., 2015; Sedikides & Wildschut, 2016, 2018). The review chapter by Sedikides et al. (2015) is the best single entry point.

**Regulatory model.** Wildschut and Sedikides (2023a) organize this as a homeostatic corrective: perturbations (negative states, threats) trigger nostalgia, which countervails them. The model extends to trait and collective nostalgia, and the authors propose causal-mediation tests for the future.

**Where the average stands.** According to its repository abstract, the 2026 meta-analysis (Weingarten et al.) pooled 1,460 effect sizes from 347 experiments for benefits (g ≈ 0.39) and found threat as a trigger at about g ≈ 0.43, with modest downward adjustment for selection bias. *Verify in the paper before quoting.* Practical consequence for you: a typical effect is small-to-moderate, so you need well-powered designs (see the workbook's power sandbox).

### Reality checks
- **Ecological validity.** Newman et al. (2020) built a scale of everyday nostalgic experiences (PINE) and found that, in daily life, state nostalgia was negatively related to well-being, with mixed same-day and negative next-day effects. Induced recollections were more positive than everyday ones. Their conclusion: nostalgia is a mixed emotion that skews positive when memories are generated on request.
- **Culture.** In 28 countries plus Hong Kong (N = 2,606), nostalgia was common, triggers varied (threat in warmer countries, sensory stimuli in more-developed countries, social gatherings in less-developed ones), and nostalgic recall raised connectedness, continuity and meaning everywhere (Hepper et al., 2024).
- **Lifespan.** Higher nostalgia proneness went with maintained or higher well-being across ages 18 to 91, and nostalgic recall buffered the wellbeing cost of limited time (Hepper et al., 2021).

## 4. Nostalgia through the lens of autobiographical memory

This is the bridge between nostalgia research and our lab's expertise.

### The memory machinery
- **Self-memory system.** Autobiographical memories are *constructed* from a hierarchy of knowledge (lifetime periods, general events, event-specific knowledge), guided by the self's current goals (Conway & Pleydell-Pearce, 2000).
- **Encoding specificity.** A cue works to the extent that it matches information encoded with the event (Tulving & Thomson, 1973). Sensory cues are, by definition, features of the original experience.
- **Sensory reactivation.** Vivid remembering reactivates sensory-specific cortex: visual regions for pictures, auditory regions for sounds, as a subset of those active during perception (Wheeler et al., 2000). A sensory nostalgia cue may therefore give a direct route into the reactivated trace.
- **Involuntary retrieval.** Cue-triggered memories that come to mind without a deliberate search are common (Berntsen, 1996), and music-evoked memories show features of involuntary retrieval, including less executive involvement (El Haj et al., 2012).

### The reminiscence bump
People disproportionately recall events from roughly ages 10 to 30: about 10 to 30 for "important events," 5 to 30 for word-cued memories and 6 to 39 in life-script studies, with narrative/identity and cultural life-script accounts best supported (68 studies; Munawar et al., 2018; see Rubin & Schulkind, 1997). The bump is tied to identity formation (Rathbone et al., 2008; Curci et al., 2024).

For sensory cues the bump is shaped by the cue:
- **Music.** Memories, recognition and liking peak for songs from participants' own youth, and also for their parents' generation (Krumhansl & Zupnick, 2013); a larger study found autobiographical salience peaking around age 14 (Jakubowski et al., 2020).
- **Smell.** Odor-cued memories clustered in the *first decade* of life, earlier than word and picture cues (Willander & Larsson, 2006). Naming the odor attenuated that peak (Willander & Larsson, 2007).
- **Consumer preferences** also peak for things popular in one's youth (Holbrook & Schindler, 1989; Schindler & Holbrook, 2003).

> **Open question:** Is nostalgia itself bump-shaped, and does the peak differ by cue modality? The existing evidence is mostly about autobiographical memory in general.

### Feeling versus accuracy
- **Fluency misattribution.** In word-list experiments where some items supported richer recollection, rich recollection biased judgments of the earlier encounter toward positive, consistent with nostalgia reflecting "a misattribution of successful remembering to a pleasant past" (Leboe & Ansons, 2006).
- **Perspective.** Nostalgic (vs ordinary) recall raised positive affect when recalled from a first-person but not a third-person perspective (N = 409; Rogers, 2020).
- **Rosy view.** The classic demonstration that evaluations of events shift over time toward a "rosy view" is Mitchell et al. (1997); I saw the title only, so read it before relying on the details.
- **What I did not find:** studies that check *nostalgic* memories against an external record of what happened. This is a clear gap for a memory lab.

### Measuring memory richness
The Autobiographical Interview separates internal (episodic) from external (semantic) details (Levine et al., 2002) and was used to show music-evoked memories have more internal and perceptual details than face-evoked memories (Belfi et al., 2016). Self-report ratings (vividness, reliving, "brought back in time") are quicker but conflate feeling and content. Text analysis such as LIWC can add affective content (de Bruijn & Bender, 2018).

### Nostalgia and memory functions
Compared with rumination and counterfactual thinking, nostalgia is more strongly linked to using memory for intimacy maintenance, teaching/informing others and self-regard, and less to reviving bitterness (Cheung et al., 2018); a similar picture holds for experimentally induced nostalgia compared with reflection and brooding (Jiang et al., 2021). For general function frameworks, see Rasmussen and Berntsen (2009).

## 5. Sensory nostalgia

### Which senses?
- In Hepper et al. (2024), "sensory stimuli" was one of three major self-reported trigger categories.
- When 256 people answered an open-ended "What makes you feel nostalgic?" question, the most frequent cues were sensory and environmental: photographs, music, media, smells, conversations and food (Zaragoza Scherman et al., 2026). That is what people *say*; it is not a test of what *works best*.

### 5.1 Smell: the Proust effect

**Evidence for a special status**
- *Not rehearsed:* odor-evoked memories were thought about and talked about less often beforehand (Rubin et al., 1984).
- *More emotional:* the same cue presented as an odor evoked more emotional and evocative memories than as an image or sound, with no difference in vividness or specificity (Herz & Schooler, 2002; Herz, 2004).
- *Older:* most odor-cued memories came from the first decade of life, and they felt more like being brought back in time (N = 93 older adults; Willander & Larsson, 2006).
- *Richer (in some designs):* odor cues yielded more detail in a double-cuing paradigm (Chu & Downes, 2002; see also Chu & Downes, 2000), and childhood-related odors produced richer memories than childhood images (N = 170; de Bruijn & Bender, 2018).
- *Summary heuristic:* Larsson et al. (2014) call the key features **LOVER**: Limbic, Old, Vivid, Emotional, Rare.

**Evidence for nuance**
- Vividness and specificity did not differ by cue form in Herz (2004).
- Odor-evoked memories of an aversive film were more detailed, unpleasant and arousing than those evoked by auditory triggers, but not than visual ones, even with matched triggers (N = 70; Toffolo et al., 2012).
- Verbal knowledge matters: naming the odor reduced the childhood peak and pleasantness, and odor-evoked memories remained more arousing than verbally cued ones (N = 72; Willander & Larsson, 2007).
- Hackländer et al. (2019) review the methods and conclude the field should move from *establishing* the effect to *explaining its causes*.

**Neural**
- Personally meaningful odors elicited larger amygdala and hippocampal responses in a five-participant fMRI study (Herz et al., 2004).
- The same autobiographical odor memory cued by the odor or its name activated overlapping recollection regions; odor cues drove more medial temporal, visual-imagery and emotion-related activity, and naming the memory activated olfactory areas (Arshamian et al., 2013).
- Gottfried et al. (2004) studied human olfactory cortex in cross-modal recognition memory (I saw the title only, not the abstract; read it for the mechanism).

**Scent-evoked nostalgia**
Participants sampled 12 scents. Nostalgia was greater for scents that were arousing, familiar and autobiographically relevant and for people high in nostalgia proneness; it predicted positive affect, self-esteem, self-continuity, optimism, social connectedness and meaning, and carried more positive emotion than non-nostalgic memories (Reid et al., 2015). Consumer work on nostalgic memories and scents (Orth & Bourrain, 2008) and a health-oriented review of odor-evoked memory (Herz, 2016) extend this to applications.

### 5.2 Music and sound
- **How often.** About 30% of song presentations evoked autobiographical memories, usually with strong, mostly positive emotion, and nostalgia was the third most common emotion (Janata et al., 2007).
- **What predicts nostalgia.** Nostalgia grew with autobiographical salience, arousal and familiarity, and with mixed positive-and-negative emotion; nostalgia proneness predicted stronger experiences (Barrett et al., 2010). A conceptual replication with self-selected nostalgic songs and musically matched familiar songs (N = 582) supported this and showed personality shaped the felt profile (Hennessy et al., 2024).
- **Everyday life.** Music-evoked memories occurred about once per day, vivid, often involuntary, with positive or mixed emotions including nostalgia (N = 31; Jakubowski & Ghosh, 2021). Music triggered more memories, more involuntary memories and more personally important memories than food cues in young and older adults (Jakubowski et al., 2023).
- **Richness.** Music memories were more vivid than face-evoked memories (Belfi et al., 2016) and involuntariness alone did not explain the advantage (Belfi et al., 2022). A recent perspective ties music's memory cueing to imagination (Margulis & Jakubowski, 2024). Older work: Schulkind et al. (1999); a mechanisms framework: Juslin and Västfjäll (2008).
- **Cross-generational effect.** Parents' music matters (Krumhansl & Zupnick, 2013; Jakubowski et al., 2020).

### 5.3 Taste and food
- Food is "a powerful elicitor of nostalgia" with a profile like other elicitors but a predominantly positive tone; it served positive affect, self-esteem, social connectedness and meaning functions (three studies, including tasting 12 flavour samples; Reid et al., 2023).
- Nostalgia for food experiences elevated comfort by strengthening social connectedness in preregistered experiments (Reid et al., 2026).
- Qualitative work found six themes of nostalgic food consumption (childhood, yearning, substitute, homesickness, special occasions, rediscovery; Vignolles & Pichon, 2014).
- Comfort foods activate relationship concepts and buffer belongingness threats in securely attached people (Troisi & Gabriel, 2011); this is about comfort food rather than nostalgia as such, but suggests a mechanism.

### 5.4 Temperature and touch
Colder days and rooms increased nostalgia, and nostalgia increased perceived warmth and tolerance of noxious cold (Zhou et al., 2012). This is the clearest example of nostalgia interacting with *interoceptive/somatosensory* signals, and a good replication target.

### 5.5 Vision, immersion and multisensory cues
- Childhood-related visual stimuli engaged hippocampus and reward regions in an fMRI study of healthy women (Oba et al., 2016).
- An EEG study of audio, visual and audiovisual nostalgic stimuli reported more state nostalgia for auditory and audiovisual than visual stimuli (Hu et al., 2025); read it critically, since the retrieved text suggests a fixed presentation order and non-matched controls.
- A virtual-reality induction produced stronger and longer-lasting felt nostalgia than the standard event reflection task, with similar benefits (Yılmaz-Özdemir et al., 2025).

### 5.6 Why cross-modal comparisons are a minefield
When studies appear to rank the senses, they often differ in:
1. **Familiarity and personal relevance** of the cue (both drive nostalgia: Barrett et al., 2010; Reid et al., 2015).
2. **Who chooses the cue** (experimenter vs participant; Hennessy et al., 2024 used self-selected cues with matched controls).
3. **Labels** (naming an odor changes the result; Willander & Larsson, 2007).
4. **Design**: within vs between subjects; cue shown before vs after a verbal retrieval (Herz & Schooler, 2002; Chu & Downes, 2002).
5. **Matching** on intensity, valence, arousal and salience (done well by Toffolo et al., 2012).
6. **Order** (fixed presentation order confounds modality with time).
7. **Sample** (older adults in Willander & Larsson, 2006; clinical samples in El Haj et al., 2018).
8. **Outcome** (nostalgia, emotion, vividness and specificity are different DVs and often dissociate; compare Herz, 2004 with de Bruijn & Bender, 2018).

## 6. Brain

A prospective review proposes four core components with candidate regions (Yang et al., 2022): self-reflection (medial prefrontal cortex, posterior cingulate, precuneus), autobiographical memory (hippocampus plus the same midline regions), emotion regulation (anterior cingulate, medial prefrontal) and reward (striatum, substantia nigra/ventral tegmental area, ventromedial prefrontal). The authors note that only a handful of neural studies existed.

| Study | Cue | Main result |
|---|---|---|
| Oba et al. (2016) | Childhood-related visual stimuli (healthy women) | Hippocampus, SN/VTA and ventral striatum; hippocampus-striatum co-activation scaled with nostalgia tendency |
| Janata (2009) | Music from participants' childhoods | Dorsal medial prefrontal cortex tracked autobiographical salience |
| Hennessy et al. (2025) | Personalized nostalgic vs familiar vs unfamiliar music (N = 57; young and older) | Default-mode, salience, reward and medial temporal engagement; older adults stronger |
| Herz et al. (2004) | Personally meaningful odor (N = 5) | Amygdala and hippocampus |
| Arshamian et al. (2013) | Odor vs word cue for the same memory | Overlapping recollection network; odor adds medial temporal, emotion and imagery regions |
| Hu et al. (2025) | Audio, visual, audiovisual (EEG) | Alpha/gamma network changes with nostalgic stimuli (see caveats) |

All of these are small studies with cue and design differences, so treat the picture as a hypothesis-generating map.

## 7. Aging and clinical work

- In three experiments with people with mild or moderate dementia (N = 27, 29, 50), nostalgia raised social connectedness, meaning, self-continuity, optimism, self-esteem and positive affect, and nostalgic participants recalled more self-referent dementia-related information (Ismail et al., 2018). A systematic review and meta-analysis of experimental evidence on nostalgia as a resource for people with dementia is Ismail et al. (2020).
- **Cues in Alzheimer's disease.** Music and odor each improved specificity, emotional experience and retrieval time in mild Alzheimer's disease, with less executive involvement (El Haj et al., 2012; El Haj et al., 2018). Odor raised arousal, reliving and positivity only in the Alzheimer's participants, not in controls (Glachet & El Haj, 2019). Positivity effects for music-evoked memories are reported by Cuddy et al. (2017).
- **Healthy aging.** Older adults recalled older, less specific, more positive, vivid and rehearsed music- and food-evoked memories (Jakubowski et al., 2023) and showed stronger responses to nostalgic music (Hennessy et al., 2025). A broad review of nostalgia in vulnerable populations: Wildschut and Sedikides (2023b, *European Review of Social Psychology*).

## 8. Methods toolkit

| Need | Options | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **Induce nostalgia** | Event reflection task (nostalgic vs ordinary event); self-selected nostalgic songs; scents; foods; VR | A VR induction beat the event reflection task on felt nostalgia (Yılmaz-Özdemir et al., 2025). Effects depend on induction and control (Leunissen et al., 2021). |
| **Control condition** | Ordinary event; recent positive event; reflection or brooding; familiar non-nostalgic music matched on features | Choice changes effect sizes (Leunissen et al., 2021; Jiang et al., 2021; Hennessy et al., 2024, 2025). |
| **Trait nostalgia** | Southampton Nostalgia Scale; Nostalgia Inventory (Batcho, 1995); PINE for everyday experiences (Newman et al., 2020) | Nostalgia proneness predicts nostalgic responses to scents and music (Reid et al., 2015; Barrett et al., 2010). |
| **State nostalgia** | Short state items as manipulation check | Use validated wording in a real study; the workbook items are practice versions. |
| **Memory richness** | Autobiographical Interview; vividness and reliving ratings; LIWC | Blind coders; word-count covariate. |
| **Cue sets** | Self-selected + matched controls; pilot rating of familiarity, arousal, personal relevance | Do this in a separate session. |
| **Ecological** | Diary/ESM with cue logging | Jakubowski & Ghosh (2021); Jakubowski et al. (2023); Newman et al. (2020). |

## 10. How to extend this search

**Databases:** PsycINFO, Web of Science, Scopus, Google Scholar, PubMed.

**Search strings to start with:**
- `nostalgi* AND (odor OR olfact* OR scent OR smell)`
- `nostalgi* AND (music OR song*) AND autobiographical`
- `"odor-evoked" OR "odour-evoked" AND autobiographical AND (cue OR modality)`
- `nostalgi* AND (vividness OR "episodic detail*" OR "Autobiographical Interview")`
- `nostalgi* AND (fMRI OR EEG OR neural)`
- `"Proust" AND (memory OR phenomenon)`

**Strategies:** forward-citation search on Reid et al. (2015), Hackländer et al. (2019), Barrett et al. (2010), Hennessy et al. (2024, 2025); backward search on the Weingarten et al. (2026) reference list; the Southampton group's publication pages for recent work; preprint servers (PsyArXiv) for work not yet in print.

**Record keeping:** keep a log of strings, databases, dates and counts (a PRISMA-style flow chart is overkill here, but a simple spreadsheet is not).
