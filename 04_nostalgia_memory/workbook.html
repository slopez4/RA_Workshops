Read like a scientist

1970s · 60–90 min

Pick three papers (tick “I’m reading this”). For each, read the abstract, methods and one figure or table, then fill the form in your own words. The guiding questions point at what to look for. Pick at least one smell or music paper and one that is about design or ecological validity.

2

Be a participant

1980s · 30 min + a week

2a · Event reflection task on yourself

The standard nostalgia induction asks people to recall a nostalgic event or an ordinary event. Do both, on separate occasions (at least an hour apart), in the order the page gives you. This is N = 1: it cannot test anything, but it shows you what participants experience, and what a control condition is for.

Wording is adapted for practice. The three state items and the other ratings are my own practice items, not a validated scale.

2b · Seven-day cue diary

Whenever you notice a nostalgic episode (a memory that comes with that bittersweet pull), log it. Aim for 5–10 entries over a week. Note whether it came unprompted or you went looking for it. Diary methods like this are used for music- and food-evoked memories (Jakubowski et al., 2023).

Think: in the literature, odor-cued memories skew early in life (Willander & Larsson, 2006), songs’ salience peaks in adolescence (Jakubowski et al., 2020), and music memories are often involuntary (Jakubowski et al., 2023). Does your chart look like that? What would you need to say anything with confidence? (More people, matched cues, better dating of memories.) Demo entries are made up to show the charts; clear them before you log real ones.

5

Power sandbox

1990s · 20 min

How many people do you need? The 2026 meta-analysis reports an average benefit around g ≈ 0.39 (Weingarten et al., 2026, as reported in its repository abstract; verify in the paper). Try it, then see what happens when the effect you care about is an interaction. These are normal-approximation numbers for teaching; use G*Power or simulation for a real plan.

An interaction (2 × 2) needs far more

Suppose you test a 2 × 2 between-subjects design (e.g., scent present/absent × music present/absent). If the interaction (difference of differences) is as large as the simple effect, you need about 4× the total sample; if it is half as large, about 16× (formula: n per cell = 4 × ((zα + zβ)/δ)² with δ in SD units).
